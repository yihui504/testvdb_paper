# Writing a Good Communication Introduction

## Core Principle
Communication reviewers want to see that the phenomenon matters and that it did not appear out of nowhere. Lead with a concrete, grounded scene, then de-naturalize it.

## What Makes It Good
### Grounded Opening: Start with something observable — a media event, a cultural shift, an audience behavior. Use specific details (dates, platforms, actors).
### De-naturalization: Show the phenomenon is historically constructed, not self-evident. Trace how it emerged, shifted, or was produced by specific conditions. This separates scholarly analysis from journalism.
### Clear Gap: Name what existing scholarship has done well. Identify precisely what it has not addressed. Frame the gap as consequential, not merely absent.
### Argument Preview: State central argument in one clear sentence. Briefly name method and theoretical lens. Indicate what paper contributes to the field.

## Language Patterns
**Opening with a scene**: "When [specific event] happened in [year], it prompted...", "Across [platform/context], [practice] has become routine, yet..."
**De-naturalization**: "This practice, however, is not inevitable. It emerged from...", "What appears natural today was, until [decade], unthinkable..."
**Gap statement**: "Existing research has examined X and Y, but has not accounted for...", "Scholars have focused on [aspect], leaving [aspect] underexplored..."
**Argument**: "This paper argues that...", "Drawing on [theory], we demonstrate that..."

## Anti-AI Rules
- No em-dashes. Use commas, periods, or semicolons instead.
- Every sentence must carry information. Cut filler.
- Think: "What do readers and reviewers need to know here?"

## Common Pitfalls
- Starting too broad ("Communication is fundamental to society")
- Treating the phenomenon as self-explanatory
- Listing what others have done without identifying a gap
- Burying the argument in the last paragraph
