# Writing a Good Law Introduction

## Core Principle
Law reviewers want doctrinal precision and practical relevance. Lead with the specific legal problem, not abstract theory.

## What Makes It Good
### Problem-Centered Opening: Start with a concrete legal problem — a disputed provision, a gap in regulation, a doctrinal inconsistency. Ground in practice: real cases, enforcement difficulties.
### Precise Problem Statement: Name the exact provision(s). Show why current law is inadequate. Identify doctrinal tension or value conflict.
### Clear Research Question: State what interpretive or normative question you address. Indicate analytical method. Preview position without fully arguing.
### Academic Modifiers in Titles: Use modifier phrases that signal analytical depth. Good: "Normative Reconstruction of...", "Doctrinal Basis for...". Bad: "Research on:", "Discussion of:", keyword-colon format.

## Language Patterns
**Opening with a legal problem**: "Article X of the [Statute] provides that... However, in practice...", "The 2020 amendment to [Law] introduced... raising the question of..."
**Problem statement**: "The current framework fails to address...", "This provision creates tension between [principle A] and [principle B]..."
**Research question**: "This paper examines whether [provision] should be interpreted as...", "This article argues that [doctrinal position] because..."

## Anti-AI Rules
- Never write "the essence of X lies not in Y but in Z" — flagged AI pattern
- Use quotation marks only for statute names and specific legal concepts, not for emphasis
- Footnotes are for citation sources only — express your own views in body text

## Common Pitfalls
- Starting too broad ("The rule of law is fundamental to society")
- No clear identification of which provisions are at issue
- Opinions hidden in footnotes instead of argued in text
- Using quotation marks for emphasis rather than for statutory terms
