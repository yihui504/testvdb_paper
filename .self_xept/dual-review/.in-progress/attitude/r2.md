# Reviewer 2: Strict/Critical Evaluation

## Summary

This paper targets documentation-implementation defects in Vector Database Management Systems (VDBMSs)—where systems silently accept inputs violating API documentation—using an LLM-derived oracle. The authors present TestVDB, a four-stage pipeline with a "dev-reviewer" agent that grounds LLM verdicts in implementation source to suppress false positives. Across 107 submitted issues, maintainers acknowledged 49 true-positive defects (15 fixed via merged PR). A 48-candidate retrospective shows 67% precision and 74% recall with source grounding versus 37% recall without it. A bidirectional probe against VDBFuzz explores complementary coverage.

The core contribution—source-grounded falsification to mitigate LLM false positives—is meaningful and technically sound. However, the evaluation has several methodological weaknesses that undermine confidence in the headline claims: (1) the minus-source ablation is not adequately isolated to credibly demonstrate source grounding's contribution; (2) cross-model reliability is poor (κ=0.14-0.51), raising questions about robustness; (3) post-hoc selection of the 3-run operating point without selection-aware confidence intervals inflates the apparent precision/recall; (4) external validity is weak, with only a single non-VDBMS case (Apache CouchDB) and no systematic testing across broader regimes; (5) the paper is constrained to a single LLM backbone (GLM-5.2), limiting generalizability; and (6) at 8 pages (excluding references), several aspects receive insufficient exposition—the threat model, the dev-reviewer's implementation details, and the VDBFuzz probe design.

I do not recommend acceptance in the current state. A revision that addresses the methodological weaknesses—particularly the ablation design, the post-hoc operating point selection, and the limited external validation—would be needed for this work to meet the standards of a top SE venue.

## Strengths

1. **Problem framing is relevant.** Documentation-implementation defects are a real and practical issue, especially in systems where silent acceptance corrupts downstream applications. The focus on defects that escape crash oracles is justified.

2. **Oracle exclusion argument (Table 1) is structured.** The paper systematically walks through why deterministic oracles (crash, differential, metamorphic, property-based, REST-API spec-derived) miss the documentation-implementation residual, leaving LLMs as the practical oracle. This clarifies the niche TestVDB occupies.

3. **Bidirectional VDBFuzz probe is conceptually sound.** Testing whether TestVDB can reach a crash-class defect by contract reasoning (v1.4.0) and whether VDBFuzz can reach a silent-accept defect (v1.18.0) explores complementary coverage. The direction-A case (integer overflow on size=$2^{63}$) demonstrates that contract reasoning can surface crash-class bugs.

4. **False-positive diagnosis is reasonable.** Identifying hallucination at extraction and self-preference at judgment as the two failure modes is a plausible decomposition of the LLM reliability problem.

## Weaknesses

### **[HIGH, fixable] Minus-source ablation is inadequately isolated**

The paper claims that "without source access, recall drops from 74% to 19%, and precision from 67% to 56%" (Section 5.2, RQ2). This is intended to isolate the contribution of source grounding. However, the ablation is confounded:

1. **The "minus-source" condition still uses the dev-reviewer.** The dev-reviewer is the agent that applies the three checks (independently reproducible, evidence sufficient, falsifiable). Disabling Step 3.5 (source grounding) still leaves the other two anchors (clean-reproduction, threat-model) active. The performance difference may be due to the threat-model anchor or interaction effects, not source grounding alone.

2. **The controlled ablation (Table 4) is on a different dataset.** Table 4 reports a three-condition ablation on a 12-FP/4-TP control set (Milvus v2.6.19, blind). The full 48-candidate retrospective includes both Milvus and Qdrant. The control ablation shows "Source-grounded alone" suppresses 9/12 FPs (75%) and "Union (source + threat)" suppresses 11/12 (91%). But the headline recall gain (37%→74%) is on the full 48-candidate set, where we do not see a cleanly isolated source-only versus threat-only comparison. The paper needs a fully crossed ablation on the same dataset to credibly isolate source grounding's contribution.

3. **The effect is vendor-specific.** The paper notes that on Milvus, recall "collapses from 80% to 5%" without source, but on Qdrant, recall is "roughly unchanged (57%)." This suggests the value of source grounding depends on where parameter validation logic lives (source vs. contract/response). This is an important caveat, but it is not reflected in the headline claims, which present source grounding as universally lifting recall.

**What's needed:** A fully crossed ablation on the same 48-candidate dataset with four configurations: (1) no dev-reviewer (single-LLM baseline), (2) dev-reviewer with only clean-reproduction anchor, (3) dev-reviewer with only source-grounded anchor, (4) dev-reviewer with both anchors. This would cleanly isolate the contribution of each component. Current evidence is insufficient to support the strong claim about source grounding's necessity.

### **[HIGH, fixable] Post-hoc operating point selection without selection-aware confidence intervals**

The 3-run union ensemble is presented as the headline operating point (67% precision, 74% recall). However, this is a post-hoc selection across four operating points: single run, 3-run union, 5-run union, 5-run majority. The paper acknowledges this ("post-hoc, justified by falsifier semantics") but does not adjust the confidence intervals for selection.

The paper reports Wilson 95% CIs for the 3-run precision ([49%, 81%]) and recall ([55%, 87%]), but notes "the Wilson CIs above do not account for this selection." A Bonferroni correction is mentioned but not applied to the headline numbers. This inflates the apparent reliability of the operating point.

Moreover, the single-run band shows "recall ranges 15--78%"—a massive variance. The any-confirmed union ensemble is chosen because it "sits at the knee of the precision-recall trade-off," but this is ex-post reasoning. A pre-registered analysis plan would have specified the operating point selection rule in advance. Without that, the CIs are anti-conservative.

**What's needed:** Either (1) apply a rigorous selection-aware correction (e.g., Bonferroni, false discovery rate) to the headline CIs and report the adjusted intervals, or (2) conduct a new evaluation with a pre-registered operating point selection rule. Current reporting overstates the reliability of the 3-run union performance.

### **[HIGH, fixable] Cross-model reliability undermines robustness claims**

Section 5.2 reports that a full independent re-run on all 48 candidates with DeepSeek and LongCat-2.0 shows "the verdict is family-specific (κ=0.14 DeepSeek, κ=0.51 LongCat vs. GLM single-run)." This is critically low inter-rater reliability. κ=0.14 is near chance; κ=0.51 is moderate but not strong for an automated system.

The paper acknowledges this in the threats to validity ("we cannot claim cross-family robustness"). However, the headline results and contributions are all based on a single LLM family (GLM-5.2). The dev-reviewer's reliability is therefore vendor-specific to GLM, not a general property of the approach.

If a practitioner using DeepSeek or LongCat would see drastically different verdicts, the approach's practical utility is limited to the GLM family. The paper does not explore why cross-model reliability is poor—is it sampling differences? Prompt sensitivity? Tokenization differences? This is an important unanswered question.

**What's needed:** A deeper analysis of why cross-model reliability is low, and either (1) a demonstration that prompt engineering or ensemble methods can stabilize cross-model performance, or (2) a clear framing that the approach is validated only for GLM-5.2 and cross-family generalization is an open question. The current abstract and introduction do not adequately caveat this limitation.

### **[MEDIUM, fixable] External validity is limited to a single non-VDBMS case**

Section 7 (Discussion) claims transferability to "REST APIs without OpenAPI coverage, configuration validation, and policy-as-code checks" on "structural grounds only." To probe this, the authors ran TestVDB on Apache CouchDB ~3.4.3.

This is only one non-VDBMS case. The pipeline extracted 5 behavioral claims from CouchDB's `_all_docs` documentation, probed each, and found that CouchDB rejected all clearly-invalid probes with 400 errors. The only silent-accept was `limit=0`, which returns an empty result set—characterized as "graceful behavior rather than a defect."

This is a minimal external validation. It does not test whether TestVDB can find real defects in non-VDBMS systems. It only tests that the pipeline runs end-to-end. The contrast with VDBMSs (where 49 defects were found) is attributed to CouchDB validating inputs more strictly, but this is a single data point.

The paper needs a stronger external validity argument. At minimum, 2-3 additional non-VDBMS targets with a broader range of documentation styles would strengthen the claim that the approach generalizes beyond VDBMSs.

**What's needed:** Either (1) expand the external validation to include 2-3 additional non-VDBMS targets and report whether defects are found, or (2) remove the broad transferability claims and frame the work as VDBMS-specific. Current evidence is insufficient to support claims about transfer to REST APIs, configuration validation, and policy-as-code.

### **[MEDIUM, fixable] Single backbone limits generalizability**

The entire evaluation uses a single LLM backbone: GLM-5.2. The cost table (Table 2) notes "~$10 per target at current API pricing" and "roughly 10^4 LLM calls," but no other models are tested in the main evaluation.

The cross-model pilot (Section 5.2) is the only evidence that other models behave differently, and it shows poor reliability. This raises a question: what if the results are specific to GLM-5.2's quirks? The paper does not explore whether different sampling parameters, temperatures, or prompt designs would stabilize results across models.

For a method that claims to use LLMs as oracles, single-model evaluation is a limitation. The paper should at least test with 2-3 different models (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5, DeepSeek) and report whether the approach's performance is model-agnostic or model-specific.

**What's needed:** A multi-model evaluation with at least 2-3 different LLMs, reporting precision/recall for each. If performance varies widely, the approach is model-dependent, and the paper should frame it as such.

### **[MEDIUM, fixable] 8-page constraint leaves key aspects under-explained**

At 365 lines (approximately 8 pages excluding references), several aspects receive insufficient exposition:

1. **The threat model anchor is not well-documented.** Section 4 mentions "optional threat-model anchor compares the candidate against maintainer-acknowledged by-design and wont-fix patterns," but the paper does not describe how these patterns are encoded, where they come from, or how they are used. Table 4 shows "Threat-model alone" suppresses 6/12 FPs (50%), but we have little insight into how this works.

2. **The dev-reviewer's implementation is opaque.** The paper describes the three checks (independently reproducible, evidence sufficient, falsifiable) at a high level, but does not explain how the dev-reviewer actually performs source-grounded falsification. Does it grep for specific patterns? Does it trace function calls? Does it use static analysis? The cost table notes "dev-reviewer source-grounding" accounts for ~50% of LLM calls, but the mechanism is not detailed.

3. **The VDBFuzz probe design is underspecified.** Section 5.3 (RQ3) describes the bidirectional probe, but we do not know how TestVDB was configured to reach the integer overflow on size=$2^{63}$. Was this a targeted test? Did the contract-formalizer extract the relevant claim from OpenAPI? The paper says "TestVDB's boundary probe reaches it as a documentation-implementation violation: the OpenAPI declares size≥1 with no maximum, so the value is documented-valid yet the implementation panics." But we don't know how TestVDB decided to probe size=$2^{63}$ specifically.

These gaps make it difficult to assess the approach's rigor and to replicate the study. A more detailed technical appendix or expanded main paper (10-12 pages) would help.

### **[LOW, fixable] κ values on cross-model pilot are buried**

The cross-model reliability results (κ=0.14 DeepSeek, κ=0.51 LongCat) are mentioned in RQ2 (Section 5.2) and in the threats to validity, but they are not given the prominence they deserve. This is a major limitation—if automated verdicts are model-specific, the approach's practical utility is constrained to a single LLM family.

The abstract and introduction do not mention this limitation. The contributions section does not caveat the dev-reviewer's reliability as GLM-specific. This obscures a critical weakness from the reader.

**What's needed:** Prominently disclose the single-model limitation in the abstract and introduction, and clearly caveat the dev-reviewer contribution as validated only for GLM-5.2 in the contributions section.

## Questions

1. **Ablation isolation:** Can you provide a fully crossed ablation on the same 48-candidate dataset with four configurations (no dev-reviewer, clean-reproduction only, source-grounded only, both anchors)? This would cleanly isolate each component's contribution.

2. **Operating point selection:** Why not pre-register the operating point selection rule? For example, "choose the any-confirmed ensemble with the highest recall while keeping precision ≥60%." This would remove the post-hoc selection concern.

3. **Cross-model reliability:** Why is cross-model reliability so poor? Is it due to sampling differences? Prompt sensitivity? Have you tried prompt engineering or temperature tuning to stabilize cross-model performance?

4. **External validation:** Do you plan to expand the CouchDB case to a more systematic external validation? What would a minimal set of additional non-VDBMS targets look like to support the transferability claims?

5. **Threat model encoding:** How are maintainer-acknowledged by-design and wont-fix patterns encoded in the threat-model anchor? Are these manually curated? Automatically extracted from issue labels?

6. **Source grounding mechanism:** How does the dev-reviewer actually perform source-grounded falsification? Does it grep for specific patterns? Does it use static analysis? Can you provide a concrete example of the dev-reviewer falsifying a candidate by reading source?

7. **VDBFuzz probe design:** How did TestVDB decide to probe size=$2^{63}$ specifically? Was this a targeted boundary test? Did the contract-formalizer extract the relevant claim, or was this manually designed?

## Scores

- **Relevance:** 4/5 - The problem is relevant to software testing, and VDBMSs are an important application domain.
- **Originality:** 3/5 - LLM-derived oracles and source-grounded falsification are not entirely new ideas, but the combination in the VDBMS context is novel.
- **Significance:** 3/5 - The 49 maintainer-acknowledged defects demonstrate practical impact, but methodological weaknesses limit confidence in the broader claims.
- **Technical Soundness:** 2/5 - The oracle exclusion argument and bidirectional probe are sound, but the ablation design, post-hoc operating point selection, and limited external validation undermine confidence.
- **Evaluation:** 2/5 - The evaluation is extensive but has critical methodological flaws (ablation isolation, post-hoc selection, cross-model reliability, external validity).
- **Clarity:** 3/5 - The writing is generally clear, but key aspects (threat model, dev-reviewer implementation, VDBFuzz probe) are under-explained due to the 8-page constraint.
- **Reproducibility:** 2/5 - The paper promises artifact release, but the limited exposition of the dev-reviewer's implementation and the VDBFuzz probe design makes replication difficult.

**Overall:** 2.7/5 - **WEAK ACCEPT**

The core idea—source-grounded falsification to mitigate LLM false positives—is meaningful. However, the evaluation has too many methodological weaknesses for me to recommend acceptance in the current state. I encourage the authors to revise and resubmit with (1) a cleaner ablation design, (2) selection-aware confidence intervals or pre-registered operating point selection, (3) a more thorough external validation, and (4) either multi-model evaluation or clear caveats about single-model limitations.
