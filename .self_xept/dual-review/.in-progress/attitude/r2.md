# Reviewer 2 (Strict)

## Overall Evaluation: 3 / 5

The paper presents TestVDB, a documentation-implementation consistency testing approach for vector database management systems (VDBMSs). The work addresses a real problem—logical bugs that escape crash-oracle fuzzers—and introduces a source-grounded falsifier to mitigate LLM false positives. While the core idea has merit and the evaluation shows practical impact (49 acknowledged defects across three VDBMSs), several weaknesses limit the strength of the claims.

## Weaknesses

### 1. Post-Hoc Operating Point Selection Undermines Statistical Claims

The paper reports "67% precision and 74% recall" as the headline result (Abstract, l.16), but this operating point (3-run union ensemble) is selected post-hoc from four alternatives tested after observing the data. The authors acknowledge this as "post-hoc, justified by falsifier semantics" (l.252), yet the accompanying Wilson 95% confidence intervals—$[49, 81]$ for precision and $[55, 87]$ for recall—do not account for this selection.

The authors briefly mention that a Bonferroni correction would widen the precision CI to roughly $[44, 84]$ and recall to $[51, 89]$ (l.252), but this is presented as a footnote rather than the primary reporting. The headline CIs in Table 3 (l.257-268) are uncorrected. A reviewer must ask: what would the precision/recall be if the pre-registered rule had been majority voting, which yields only 26% recall (l.265)? The paper presents the 3-run union as the operating point "at the knee of the precision-recall trade-off" (l.252) after seeing all four curves. This is a form of p-hacking in the evaluation methodology itself—the algorithm was tuned to the test set.

The fix is straightforward: either pre-register the operating point before the retrospective, or report the full family of operating points and acknowledge the selection as a limitation. Presenting post-hoc optimization as the headline result with uncorrected CIs is misleading.

### 2. Insufficient External Validation Beyond VDBMSs

The paper claims transferability to "structurally similar documentation regimes" (l.351) such as "REST APIs without OpenAPI coverage, configuration validation, and policy-as-code checks," but provides only one weak external case study: Apache CouchDB 3.4.3 (l.351). The CouchDB evaluation finds exactly zero defects—the only silent-accept observed (`limit=0` returning empty results) is deemed "a graceful behavior rather than a defect" (l.351).

This is not an external validation. It is a sanity check that the pipeline runs end-to-end on a non-VDBMS target. The authors acknowledge this as "probing method portability rather than defect detection" (l.351), which undermines the claim that the approach generalizes beyond VDBMSs. A true external validation would require at least one non-VDBMS case study where defects are actually found and acknowledged.

The external validity section (l.330) states that "Transfer to documentation regimes beyond VDBMSs... is claimed on structural grounds only and is not empirically tested; even one non-VDBMS case study would strengthen the claim." The paper presents exactly one such case study, and it finds zero defects. The external validity claim remains weak.

### 3. Cross-Family Generalization Is an Open Question Presented as a Strength

The abstract presents cross-model evaluation as evidence: "These results use a single LLM backbone (GLM-5.2); cross-family generalization is an open question" (l.16). This reads like a limitation, but the framing in the results section presents the cross-model pilot as evidence of robustness: "A cross-model pilot with DeepSeek on six candidates sharing evidence artifacts reached $\kappa = 1.0$" (l.290). The text then buries the critical caveat: "a full independent re-run on all 48 candidates... shows the verdict is family-specific ($\kappa = 0.14$ DeepSeek, $\kappa = 0.51$ LongCat vs.\ GLM single-run)" (l.290-291).

The abstract states "cross-family generalization is an open question" (l.16), but the Related Work section on LLM-as-judge reliability (l.342-343) cites self-preference bias as "established for general text evaluation" and notes that "we observe the analogous bias in the test-oracle pipeline" (l.343). The connection is not fully drawn: if the dev-reviewer is supposed to break self-preference by using implementation source as ground truth (l.43), why does cross-family generalization remain poor? The paper presents source-grounded falsification as the solution to self-preference, but the cross-model results suggest the problem persists.

The disconnect between the pilot ($\kappa = 1.0$ on 6 candidates) and the full re-run ($\kappa = 0.14$ on 48 candidates) is a red flag. The paper should foreground this as a primary limitation rather than tucking it into the middle of the results paragraph. The current framing misleads readers about the robustness of the approach across model families.
